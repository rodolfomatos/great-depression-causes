## Primary Sources

### Friedman, M. & Schwartz, A.J. (1963). *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960*. Princeton University Press.
**Role**: Foundational monetary interpretation. Money supply causation.
**Claims Supported**:
- M1 contraction −33% 1929–33 (Table 1, Ch. 7)
- 9,096 bank suspensions 1930–33 (Ch. 7, Table 7.1)
- Fed government securities flat 1929–32 (Table 7.2, Ch. 7)
- Monetary base growth +1.2%/yr vs. +6% normal (Ch. 7)
- Rural bank failures 5,712 1921–29 (Ch. 7)
- Margin debt $27B → $2B (Ch. 7)
- Deposits in failed banks $6.8B (Ch. 7)
- Commercial bank loans −52% (Ch. 7)
**Key Chapters**: Ch. 7 "The Great Contraction", Ch. 8 "Banking Crises"
**Debate Position**: Monetary cause = *the* cause; Fed incompetence.

---

### Eichengreen, B. (1992). *Golden Fetters: The Gold Standard and the Great Depression, 1919–1939*. Oxford University Press.
**Role**: International transmission mechanism; gold standard as binding constraint.
**Claims Supported**:
- 44 countries on gold 1929 → 12 by 1936 (Ch. 2)
- France + US = 60% gold reserves 1929 (Table 2.1, Ch. 2)
- UK gold reserves loss £200M 1929–31 (Ch. 4)
- Recovery timing correlation r=0.85 (exit gold → recovery) (Ch. 7, with Sachs)
- Competitive devaluation avoidance / collective action problem (Ch. 5)
- Fed chose gold over banks Sep 1931 (Ch. 4)
**Key Chapters**: Ch. 2 "The Gold Standard Restored", Ch. 4 "The Crisis of 1931", Ch. 5 "The Gold Standard and Recovery", Ch. 7 (with Sachs) "Exchange Rates and Economic Recovery"
**Debate Position**: Gold standard = *the* cause; explains timing, depth, sync.

---

### Bernanke, B.S. (1983). "Nonmonetary Effects of the Financial Crisis in the Propagation of the Great Depression." *American Economic Review* 73(3): 257–276.
**Role**: Credit channel / nonmonetary transmission via bank failures.
**Claims Supported**:
- Bank credit/GDP ratio −40% 1929–33 (Table 1)
- Bond spreads ↑ for low-rated firms (Table 2)
- Bank-dependent sectors contracted more (construction, retail)
- Nonmonetary channel ≈ 1/3 of output decline
- Borrower-lender relationship destruction mechanism
**Debate Position**: Complements Friedman & Schwartz; credit channel independent of money supply.

---

### Temin, P. (1976). *Did Monetary Forces Cause the Great Depression?* Norton.
**Role**: Spending hypothesis / autonomous consumption alternative.
**Claims Supported**:
- Autonomous consumption drop caused output fall (Ch. 3)
- Money supply endogenous (Fed reacted, didn't cause)
- Agricultural depression independent structural cause (Ch. 2)
- Stock crash passive (anticipated contraction)
- Farm prices −52% 1920–21 (Ch. 2)
**Debate Position**: Monetarism wrong; spending decline = cause.

---

### Kindleberger, C.P. (1973). *The World in Depression, 1929–1939*. University of California Press.
**Role**: Systemic international view; hegemonic stability theory.
**Claims Supported**:
- World trade volume −66% 1929–34 (Ch. 8)
- Germany Reichsbank rate 7% → 15% (Ch. 6)
- Margin debt $27B → $2B (Ch. 3)
- Hegemon (UK) unable, US unwilling to stabilize system (Ch. 1)
- Smoot-Hawley critical error (Ch. 8)
**Debate Position**: Systemic failure; no lender of last resort internationally.

---

### Romer, C.D. (1990). "The Great Crash and the Onset of the Great Depression." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 105(3): 597–624.
**Role**: Consumption/uncertainty channel; crash as independent shock.
**Claims Supported**:
- Consumer durables −76% real 1929–32 (Table 2)
- Nonfarm residential investment −84% real (Table 1)
- Crash → uncertainty → consumption drop (not wealth effect primarily)
- Consumption fell *before* income (Table 3)
- Top 5% held 75% equities; MPC out of wealth 0.04–0.06
**Debate Position**: Crash matters via uncertainty, not just wealth; challenges Temin.

---

### Fisher, I. (1933). "The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions." *Econometrica* 1(4): 337–357.
**Role**: Deflation spiral mechanism.
**Claims Supported**:
- Deflation → real debt burden ↑ → defaults → fire sales → further deflation
- 9-step debt-deflation sequence
- Over-indebtedness + deflation = great depressions
**Debate Position**: Mechanism linking monetary contraction to real collapse.

---

### Irwin, D.A. (1998). "Higher Tariffs, Lower Revenues? Analyzing the Fiscal Aspects of the Great Depression." *NBER Working Paper* 6479. Also *American Economic Review* 1998.
**Role**: Smoot-Hawley quantitative assessment.
**Claims Supported**:
- Avg dutiable tariff 40% → 59% (Table 1)
- US imports −66%, exports −61% 1929–33
- Agricultural exports −72% (Table 4)
- 25+ countries retaliated within 2 years
- Counterfactual: tariffs at 1929 levels → exports 15% higher, GDP 0.5–1% higher
**Debate Position**: Smoot-Hawley worsened but didn't cause trade collapse.

---

### Eichengreen, B. & Sachs, J. (1985). "Exchange Rates and Economic Recovery in the 1930s." *Journal of Economic History* 45(4): 925–946.
**Role**: Cross-country evidence: leaving gold → recovery.
**Claims Supported**:
- Countries leaving gold early recovered 2–3 years earlier (Table 2)
- Recovery timing correlation r=0.85
- Gold bloc (France, Belgium, Poland) stagnated until 1936
**Debate Position**: Gold standard exit = necessary condition for recovery.

---

### Olney, M.L. (1989). "When Your Word Is Not Enough: Race, Collateral, and Household Credit." *Journal of Economic History* 49(2): 409–431. Also *Avoiding Default* (1999).
**Role**: Household credit / installment buying 1920s.
**Claims Supported**:
- Installment credit $7.2B ≈10% GDP 1929 (Table 1)
- 60% furniture, 75% autos on installment
- Household debt/disposable income ~80%
- Credit saturation → consumption cliff
**Debate Position**: Credit-mediated demand masked inequality.

---

### Piketty, T. & Saez, E. (2003). "Income Inequality in the United States, 1913–1998." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 118(1): 1–39.
**Role**: Top income shares data.
**Claims Supported**:
- Top 1% share 23.9% 1928 (Table A1)
- Bottom 90% share 50.7% 1928 (Table A1)
**Debate Position**: Inequality peak 1928; structural demand deficiency.

---

### Alston, L.J. (1983). "Farm Foreclosures in the United States During the Interwar Period." *Journal of Economic History* 43(4): 885–903.
**Role**: Farm mortgage debt / foreclosure data.
**Claims Supported**:
- Farm mortgage debt $9.2B ≈200% farm income 1929
- Foreclosure rates 1920s–30s
**Debate Position**: Structural farm debt crisis; global supply shock.

---

### Hamilton, J.D. (1988). "Role of the International Gold Standard in Propagating the Great Depression." *Contemporary Policy Issues* 6(2): 67–89.
**Role**: Gold standard not binding for US (large gold stock).
**Claims Supported**:
- US gold stock large; Fed *could* have eased
- Gold standard constraint overstated for US
**Debate Position**: Challenges Eichengreen on US autonomy.

---

### Crucini, M.J. & Kahn, J.A. (1996). "Tariffs and Aggregate Economic Activity: Lessons from the Great Depression." *Journal of Monetary Economics* 38(3): 427–467.
**Role**: Tariff uncertainty channel.
**Claims Supported**:
- Tariff uncertainty (not level) caused investment delay
- Firms waited for policy clarity
**Debate Position**: Uncertainty > level for investment impact.

---

### Kumhof, M., Rancière, R. & Winant, P. (2015). "Inequality, Leverage, and Crises." *American Economic Review* 105(3): 1217–1245.
**Role**: Modern inequality → debt → crisis framework.
**Claims Supported**:
- Inequality → household debt → financial fragility (model)
- Endogenous financial deepening
**Debate Position**: Structural link inequality to crisis (post-hoc theoretical).
