## Cause 1: Stock Market Crash of 1929

### Economic Mechanism
The crash transmitted shock to the real economy through **three channels**: (1) **Wealth effect** — household financial wealth destruction reduced consumption (Modigliani-Brumberg life-cycle hypothesis); (2) **Investment uncertainty** — Tobin's q collapsed as equity values fell below replacement cost, freezing capital expenditure; (3) **Credit contraction** — margin loans called in, forcing liquidation and reducing broker lending capacity, which spilled to commercial banks via correspondent networks.

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| DJIA peak-to-trough decline | −89.2% (381 → 41) | Sep 1929 → Jul 1932 | Standard & Poor's / Historical Statistics [page uncertain] |
| Household financial wealth loss | ≈$30 billion (≈30% of 1929 GDP) | 1929–1932 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |
| Nonfarm residential investment | −84% (real) | 1929–1933 | Romer 1990, Table 1 [page uncertain] |
| Consumer durables expenditure | −76% (real) | 1929–1932 | Romer 1990, Table 2 [page uncertain] |
| Margin debt outstanding | −92% ($27B → $2B) | Oct 1929 → 1932 | Kindleberger 1973, Ch. 3 [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Wealth → Consumption**: Top 5% held ~75% of equities; their marginal propensity to consume out of wealth ~0.04–0.06 (Romer 1990). $30B loss → ~$1.2–1.8B annual consumption drag.
2. **Tobin's q → Investment**: q = market value / replacement cost. q < 1 from 1930 onward → no new equity-financed investment. Business fixed investment fell 89% real 1929–33.
3. **Margin Loan Liquidation → Bank Stress**: Brokers' loans fell from $6.8B (Sep 1929) to $0.6B (1932). NY banks lost correspondent balances; forced securities sales depressed bond prices further.

### Counterfactual Assessment
**Without the crash** (assuming 1929 peak maintained): Consumption would have been ~2–3% higher 1930–32; investment might have declined less severely (but still faced overcapacity in autos/construction). **However**: The crash was largely *symptomatic* of overvaluation (P/E > 30) and Fed tightening 1928–29 (Temin 1976). A "soft landing" was unlikely given monetary stance.

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Amplifies Banking Crises (Cause 2)**: Margin liquidation → broker failures → correspondent bank runs (Bank of United States, Dec 1930).
- **Amplified by Fed Policy (Cause 3)**: Fed raised discount rate 1928–29 to curb speculation; crash was policy-induced.
- **Amplifies Inequality/Subconsumption (Cause 6)**: Wealth destruction concentrated at top, but middle-class margin buyers wiped out → consumption collapse broader.

### Known Debates
- **Temin (1976) vs. Romer (1990)**: Temin argues crash was *passive* (anticipated contraction); Romer shows consumption fell *before* income via uncertainty channel.
- **Friedman & Schwartz (1963)**: Crash matters mainly via banking system impact, not direct wealth effect.
- **Cause vs. Symptom**: If crash is symptom of monetary tightening, counting it as separate cause double-counts.

## Cause 2: Banking Crises (1930–1933)

### Economic Mechanism
**Four waves of bank failures** (Nov 1930, Mar 1931, Sep 1931, Feb–Mar 1933) transmitted shock via: (1) **Deposit destruction** — uninsured deposits lost → money supply contraction (Friedman & Schwartz monetary channel); (2) **Credit intermediation collapse** — relationship lending severed → firms couldn't roll over working capital (Bernanke 1983 nonmonetary channel); (3) **Deflationary spiral** — fire sales of assets → price declines → higher real debt burdens (Fisher 1933); (4) **Payment system disruption** — check clearing impaired → velocity fall.

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| Banks suspended | 9,096 (of 24,000) | 1930–1933 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7, Table 7.1 [page uncertain] |
| Deposits in failed banks | $6.8 billion | 1930–1933 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |
| Money supply (M1) contraction | −33% ($26.5B → $17.7B) | 1929–1933 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Table 1 [page uncertain] |
| Commercial bank loans | −52% ($35.8B → $17.2B) | 1929–1933 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |
| Bank credit / GDP ratio | −40% | 1929–1933 | Bernanke 1983, Table 1 [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Monetary Channel (Friedman & Schwartz)**: Fed failed as lender of last resort → currency/deposit ratio ↑ + reserve/deposit ratio ↑ → money multiplier ↓ → M1 ↓ 33%. This *caused* the Great Contraction.
2. **Nonmonetary Credit Channel (Bernanke 1983)**: Bank failures destroyed borrower-lender relationships. Small/medium firms (bank-dependent) faced credit rationing. Evidence: Bond spreads ↑ for low-rated firms; bank-dependent sectors (construction, retail) contracted more.
3. **Debt-Deflation (Fisher 1933)**: Deflation 10%/yr → real debt burden ↑ → defaults ↑ → collateral values ↓ → further bank losses → credit contraction.
4. **Velocity Collapse**: Check volume ↓ 40% 1929–33 (payment system disruption); M1 velocity fell from 1.7 to 1.1.

### Counterfactual Assessment
**With FDIC + lender-of-last-resort** (counterfactual 1930–33): Friedman & Schwartz estimate M1 would have fallen ~5% not 33%. Bernanke estimates nonmonetary channel accounts for ~1/3 of output decline. Combined: Great Depression might have been a severe recession (GDP −10% not −30%).

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Amplified by Fed Policy (Cause 3)**: Fed refused to lend to illiquid but solvent banks (St. Louis Fed 1931); raised rates Sep 1931 to defend gold.
- **Amplifies Gold Standard (Cause 4)**: Bank failures → gold outflows (foreigners lose confidence) → Fed forced to raise rates → more failures.
- **Amplified by Agricultural Depression (Cause 7)**: Rural banks failed first (1920s farm debt); contagion to urban correspondents.
- **Amplifies Trade Collapse (Cause 5)**: Trade finance (acceptances) evaporated → export/import letters of credit unavailable.

### Known Debates
- **Friedman & Schwartz (monetary) vs. Bernanke (nonmonetary)**: F&S: money supply is *the* cause; Bernanke: credit channel adds independent explanatory power.
- **Temin (1976)**: Spending hypothesis — autonomous consumption drop caused both output fall and bank failures (reverse causality).
- **Calomiris & Mason (2003)**: Failures were *fundamentally* insolvent, not just illiquid; Fed lending would have delayed inevitable.

## Cause 3: Federal Reserve Contractionary Policy

### Economic Mechanism
The Fed's actions (and inactions) operated through: (1) **1928–29 tightening** — discount rate 3.5% → 6% to curb "speculative excess" (Adolph Miller view); (2) **1930–32 failure to ease** — discount rate cut to 2.5% by 1931 but *real* rate high due to deflation; (3) **Sep 1931 rate hike** — 1.5% → 3.5% to defend gold standard after UK departure; (4) **No open market operations** — government securities portfolio flat 1930–32 (should have expanded to offset gold outflows).

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| Discount rate (NY Fed) | 6% → 1.5% → 3.5% | 1929 → 1931 → Sep 1931 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |
| Real discount rate (ex post) | >10% | 1930–1932 | Calculated: nominal 2.5% − (−10% deflation) |
| Fed government securities | ~$600M (flat) | 1929–1932 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Table 7.2 [page uncertain] |
| Monetary base growth | +1.2%/yr (vs. +6% normal) | 1929–1933 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |
| Excess reserves | Negative (borrowed reserves) | 1930–1932 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Monetary Base → Money Supply**: Fed controlled base but let multiplier collapse. Required: $1B OMO/year to offset gold outflows + currency drain. Actual: near zero.
2. **Real Interest Rate Channel**: Deflation 10%/yr + nominal 2.5% = real rate 12.5%. Investment demand highly interest-elastic (Keynes); user cost of capital prohibitive.
3. **Expectations Channel**: Fed inaction signaled no inflation target → deflationary expectations entrenched → delayed spending.
4. **International Channel**: Sep 1931 rate hike attracted gold but crushed domestic credit; Eichengreen (1992): "the Fed chose gold over banks."

### Counterfactual Assessment
**If Fed had pursued aggressive OMO 1930–32** (Friedman & Schwartz counterfactual): $1B/year securities purchases → monetary base stable → M1 fall limited to ~5% → price level stable → no debt-deflation → banking crises contained. **Eichengreen caveat**: Gold standard constrained Fed; OMO might have triggered gold outflow and forced devaluation *earlier* (which would have been expansionary).

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Amplified Stock Crash (Cause 1)**: 1928–29 tightening pricked bubble.
- **Amplifies Banking Crises (Cause 2)**: Refused lender-of-last-resort; Sep 1931 hike triggered wave 3.
- **Amplified by Gold Standard (Cause 4)**: Gold standard *forced* Sep 1931 hike; Fed had no autonomy.
- **Amplifies Trade Collapse (Cause 5)**: High rates → strong dollar → exports uncompetitive.

### Known Debates
- **Friedman & Schwartz (1963)**: Fed *incompetence* — knew better, failed to act.
- **Eichengreen (1992)**: Fed *constrained* — gold standard made easing impossible without devaluation.
- **Temin (1976)**: Fed *passive* — money supply endogenous; Fed reacted to, didn't cause, contraction.
- **Romer & Romer (2013)**: Narrative evidence — Fed *did* respond to inflation fears (wrongly); not pure inaction.

## Cause 4: Gold Standard Transmission

### Economic Mechanism
The **interwar gold standard** (1925–31) transmitted depression globally via: (1) **Fixed exchange rates** — no independent monetary policy (Mundell-Fleming trilemma); (2) **Gold flows as adjustment mechanism** — deficit countries *must* contract to stem outflow; (3) **Asymmetric adjustment** — surplus countries (France, US) sterilized inflows; deficit countries (UK, Germany) bore full burden; (4) **Confidence contagion** — bank failures → gold convertibility fears → capital flight → further contraction.

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| Countries on gold standard | 44 (peak) → 12 | 1929 → 1936 | Eichengreen 1992, Ch. 2 [page uncertain] |
| Global gold reserves concentration | France + US = 60% | 1929 | Eichengreen 1992, Table 2.1 [page uncertain] |
| UK gold reserves loss | −£200M | 1929–1931 | Eichengreen 1992, Ch. 4 [page uncertain] |
| Germany Reichsbank discount rate | 7% → 15% | 1929 → 1931 | Kindleberger 1973, Ch. 6 [page uncertain] |
| Recovery timing correlation | r = 0.85 (exit gold → recovery) | 1931–1935 | Eichengreen & Sachs 1985, Table 2 [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Deflationary Bias**: Surplus countries (US, France) sterilized gold inflows (didn't expand money); deficit countries contracted. World monetary gold stock ↑ 30% 1929–32 but *effective* money supply ↓.
2. **Competitive Devaluation Avoidance**: No country wanted to leave first (beggar-thy-neighbor fear); collective action problem → all stayed too long.
3. **Financial Contagion**: Austrian Creditanstalt failure (May 1931) → German bank runs → UK gold outflow → UK leaves gold (Sep 1931) → US gold outflow → Fed hikes (Sep 1931).
4. **Expectations Anchor**: Gold standard anchored *deflationary* expectations (price level must return to parity); leaving allowed reflation.

### Counterfactual Assessment
**If all major countries left gold 1929** (coordinated): No deflationary bias; monetary autonomy → countercyclical policy possible. Eichengreen & Sachs (1985): Countries leaving early (UK 1931, Sweden 1931, US 1933) recovered 2–3 years earlier than gold bloc (France, Belgium, Poland until 1936). **Counterfactual cost**: Earlier exit might have caused sharper initial currency crises.

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Forces Fed Policy (Cause 3)**: Fed *had* to hike Sep 1931 to stem gold outflow; no choice under gold standard rules.
- **Amplifies Banking Crises (Cause 2)**: Gold outflows = reserve losses → banks fail → more gold outflows (vicious cycle).
- **Amplifies Trade Collapse (Cause 5)**: Fixed rates + deflation = real exchange rate misalignment → trade imbalances → protectionism.
- **Amplified by Inequality (Cause 6)**: Surplus countries' sterilization reflected domestic inequality (high savings, low consumption) — structural, not just policy.

### Known Debates
- **Eichengreen (1992) "Golden Fetters"**: Gold standard is *the* cause; explains timing, depth, international sync.
- **Friedman & Schwartz (1963)**: Gold standard is *constraint*; Fed *could* have done more within rules (OMO sterilized).
- **Temin (1989)**: "The gold standard was a policy choice" — countries *chose* to stay on; not exogenous.
- **Hamilton (1988)**: Gold standard *not* binding for US (large gold stock); Fed choices domestic.

## Cause 5: Smoot-Hawley Tariff & Trade Collapse

### Economic Mechanism
**Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act (Jun 1930)** raised average dutiable tariff from 40% to 59% on 20,000+ items. Transmission: (1) **Direct export destruction** — foreign retaliation → US exports plummet; (2) **Import substitution failure** — domestic industries couldn't replace imports (capital goods, raw materials); (3) **Income reduction abroad** — trading partners' income fell → further reduced demand for US exports; (4) **Financial channel** — trade finance collapse (acceptances) → letter of credit unavailability.

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| Average dutiable tariff rate | 40% → 59% | 1929 → 1932 | Irwin 1998, Table 1 [page uncertain] |
| US imports (value) | −66% ($4.4B → $1.5B) | 1929–1933 | Historical Statistics [page uncertain] |
| US exports (value) | −61% ($5.4B → $2.1B) | 1929–1933 | Historical Statistics [page uncertain] |
| World trade volume | −66% | 1929–1934 | Kindleberger 1973, Ch. 8 [page uncertain] |
| US agricultural exports | −72% (value) | 1929–1933 | Irwin 1998, Table 4 [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Retaliation Spiral**: 25+ countries retaliated within 2 years (Canada, France, Spain, Italy). US exports to Canada fell 55% 1930–32.
2. **Import Composition**: 60% of US imports were raw materials/semi-manufactures (rubber, tin, coffee, silk) — *no domestic substitute*. Tariff raised input costs for US manufacturers.
3. **Agricultural Devastation**: Cotton, wheat, tobacco exports collapsed. Farm income ↓ 60% 1929–32 → rural bank failures (Cause 7).
4. **Trade Finance Collapse**: Bankers' acceptances (trade finance instrument) fell 80% 1929–33. Without acceptances, exporters couldn't finance shipments.

### Counterfactual Assessment
**Without Smoot-Hawley** (Irwin 1998 simulation): Tariffs at 1929 levels → US exports ~15% higher 1931–33; GDP ~0.5–1% higher. **But**: World trade would still have collapsed ~50% due to income collapse + protectionism elsewhere. Smoot-Hawley *worsened* but didn't *cause* trade collapse.

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Amplifies Agricultural Depression (Cause 7)**: Export markets destroyed for cotton/wheat → farm prices ↓ → rural defaults.
- **Amplified by Gold Standard (Cause 4)**: Fixed rates prevented depreciation that would have offset tariffs; deflation raised real tariff burden.
- **Amplifies Banking Crises (Cause 2)**: Trade finance collapse → acceptance market frozen → banks holding acceptances took losses.
- **Amplifies Inequality (Cause 6)**: Tariffs are regressive (raise consumer prices); hurt low-income households disproportionately.

### Known Debates
- **Irwin (1998) vs. Kindleberger (1973)**: Irwin: Smoot-Hawley minor contributor (trade already collapsing); Kindleberger: critical policy error that turned recession into depression.
- **Eichengreen (1989)**: Trade collapse primarily *income-driven* (GDP ↓ → imports ↓), not tariff-driven.
- **Crucini & Kahn (1996)**: Tariff uncertainty (not level) caused investment delay — firms waited for clarity.

## Cause 6: Inequality & Subconsumption

### Economic Mechanism
**Structural demand deficiency** from skewed income distribution: (1) **High savings at top** — top 1% saved ~30% of income; bottom 90% saved near zero; (2) **Credit-mediated demand** — 1920s consumer credit (installment buying) masked insufficient wages; (3) **Investment misallocation** — capacity built for high-income demand (luxury, autos) not mass market; (4) **Debt fragility** — household debt/service ratio high; any income shock → default cascade.

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| Top 1% income share | 23.9% | 1928 | Piketty & Saez 2003, Table A1 [page uncertain] |
| Bottom 90% income share | 50.7% | 1928 | Piketty & Saez 2003, Table A1 [page uncertain] |
| Installment credit outstanding | $7.2B (≈10% GDP) | 1929 | Olney 1989, Table 1 [page uncertain] |
| Household debt / disposable income | ~80% | 1929 | Olney 1989 [page uncertain] |
| Auto production (peak) | 5.3M units | 1929 → 1.3M (1932) | Romer 1990 [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Consumption Function Shift**: High MPC at bottom (≈0.9) vs. low MPC at top (≈0.6). Redistribution from bottom to top → aggregate consumption ↓.
2. **Credit Saturation**: By 1929, 60% of furniture, 75% of autos bought on installment. Saturation → consumption cliff when credit tightened.
3. **Investment Overcapacity**: Auto capacity 6M; housing starts 1925 peak 937K → 1933 93K. Malinvestment from misread demand signals.
4. **Debt-Deflation Trigger**: Deflation ↑ real debt burden → defaults on installment loans → finance company failures → credit contraction.

### Counterfactual Assessment
**With 1920 income distribution** (top 1% ≈15%): Aggregate consumption ~5% higher 1929–30. Might have delayed but not prevented downturn (monetary shock dominant per Friedman & Schwartz). **Romer (1990)**: Autonomous consumption drop (uncertainty) matters more than inequality *per se*.

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Amplified by Stock Crash (Cause 1)**: Wealth destruction hit middle-class margin buyers (not just top 1%) → consumption collapse broader.
- **Amplifies Banking Crises (Cause 2)**: Installment loan defaults → finance company failures → commercial bank correspondent losses.
- **Amplified by Agricultural Depression (Cause 7)**: Rural incomes already depressed 1920s; no buffer for 1930s shock.
- **Amplifies Trade Collapse (Cause 5)**: Low domestic demand → export dependence → trade collapse hits harder.

### Known Debates
- **Romer (1990)**: Crash → uncertainty → consumption drop (not inequality).
- **Temin (1976)**: Spending hypothesis — autonomous consumption decline *is* the cause; inequality is background.
- **Galbraith (1954)**: "Bad distribution of income" — structural cause.
- **Modern view (Kumhof, Rancière, Winant 2015)**: Inequality → household debt → crisis (endogenous financial deepening).

## Cause 7: Agricultural Depression (1920s Origin)

### Economic Mechanism
**Pre-existing sectoral depression** transmitted to macroeconomy via: (1) **Farm income collapse** — prices fell 50% 1920–21, never recovered; (2) **Rural bank failures** — 5,000+ banks failed 1921–29 (mostly small, agricultural); (3) **Debt overhang** — farm mortgages at 1920 prices unserviceable at 1929 prices; (4) **Migration & urban unemployment** — displaced farmers → urban labor surplus → wage pressure.

### Quantitative Impact Metrics

| Metric | Value | Period | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|--------|
| Farm product prices | −52% (index) | 1920–1921 | Temin 1976, Ch. 2 [page uncertain] |
| Farm mortgage debt | $9.2B (≈200% farm income) | 1929 | Alston 1983 [page uncertain] |
| Rural bank failures (pre-1929) | 5,712 | 1921–1929 | Friedman & Schwartz 1963, Ch. 7 [page uncertain] |
| Farm population share | 25% of US population | 1930 | Census 1930 |
| Net farm income | −60% (nominal) | 1929–1932 | USDA Historical Statistics [page uncertain] |

### Transmission Channels
1. **Rural Bank Failure Pipeline**: Small unit banks (no branching) tied to local farm economy. Farm defaults → bank insolvency → depositors lose savings → local credit collapse. 1920s failures *predated* 1930s waves.
2. **Correspondent Contagion**: Rural banks deposited reserves in urban correspondents. Rural failures → urban reserve losses → urban bank strain (e.g., Bank of United States had heavy agricultural exposure).
3. **Deflationary Anchor**: Farm prices led general deflation (flexible prices). Farm price decline 1920–21 → wholesale prices ↓ → expectations of further decline.
4. **Labor Market Spillover**: 6M left farms 1920s; urban unemployment already elevated pre-1929 (≈5% vs. 3% 1920s avg).

### Counterfactual Assessment
**With 1920s farm price support** (McNary-Haugen bills vetoed by Coolidge): Farm income stable → rural banks solvent → 5,000+ pre-1929 failures avoided → banking system more resilient 1930–33. **But**: Global grain surplus (Argentina, Canada, Australia) made price support costly; would have required export subsidies or production controls.

### Interactions with Other Causes
- **Amplifies Banking Crises (Cause 2)**: First wave (Nov 1930) centered in agricultural regions (Missouri, Indiana, Illinois).
- **Amplified by Trade Collapse (Cause 5)**: Smoot-Hawley destroyed cotton/wheat exports → farm prices ↓ further.
- **Amplifies Inequality (Cause 6)**: Rural poverty = 25% of population with near-zero income → drag on aggregate demand.
- **Amplified by Gold Standard (Cause 4)**: Deflation raised real mortgage burden; no monetary relief.

### Known Debates
- **Temin (1976)**: Agricultural depression *independent* cause; not monetary.
- **Friedman & Schwartz (1963)**: Rural bank failures *part of* monetary contraction; Fed should have prevented.
- **Alston (1983)**: Farm mortgage crisis *structural* (technological change + global supply); not policy-caused.
- **Romer (1990)**: Agriculture too small (10% GDP) to drive macro; more symptom than cause.
